Sleep problems
Sleep problems can be hard to cope with, especially when they start to affect your everyday life.
A few sleepless nights are usually nothing to worry about. But if poor sleep happens often, it can start to affect how you feel and get through the day.
In this guide, you can learn how much sleep you need, how to spot sleep problems and what can cause them.
How much sleep do adults need?
Most healthy adults need around 7 to 9 hours of sleep a night, although the exact amount can be different for each person.
Your age, health and daily routine can all affect how much sleep you need. Children and teenagers usually need more sleep than adults.
How do you know if you're getting enough sleep?
How you feel during the day can help you know if you are getting enough sleep.
You may be getting enough sleep if you:
wake up feeling refreshed
have enough energy to get through the day
can focus on everyday tasks
You may not be getting enough sleep if you:
find it hard to wake up or start your day
feel very tired, irritable or sleepy during the day
find it hard to focus or cope with everyday tasks
Signs and symptoms of sleep problems
Sleep problems can affect you in different ways. You may:
find it difficult to fall asleep
lie awake for long periods at night
wake up several times during the night
wake up early and not be able to get back to sleep
When is poor sleep a problem?
A few nights of bad sleep are usually nothing to worry about. However, if you regularly do not get enough sleep, you may feel tired, irritable or less able to cope.
Longer-term sleep problems can also make daily tasks feel harder. They may affect your mood, appetite, relationships and social life.
This is because sleep gives your mind and body time to rest and repair. Getting enough sleep can support your mood, energy, immune system and overall health.
Not getting enough sleep is sometimes called sleep deprivation or sleeplessness.
What is insomnia?
Many people have poor sleep from time to time. But if you regularly find it hard to fall asleep, stay asleep or wake up too early, you may have insomnia.
Insomnia can last for a short time, or continue for months or years.
Changing your sleep habits can often help. If this does not help, or you think you may have insomnia, speak to a GP.
Visit the NHS page on insomnia for more information.
How to get better sleep
Sleep problems can take time to improve, but small changes can help.
A regular routine can help your body get used to sleeping better. Try to go to bed and wake up at around the same time each day, and give yourself time to relax before bed.
There are also simple things you can try, such as making your bedroom more comfortable, avoiding caffeine late in the day and putting your phone away before bed.

Causes of sleep problems and solutions
There are many reasons why you might not sleep well. There are also things that may help.
Stress, anxiety or worry
When you feel stressed or anxious, your body may feel more alert. Your mind may also be full of worrying thoughts.
What to do now
Learn a simple way to deal with worries that may be keeping you awake from our video: tackling your worries.
Your bedroom or surroundings
Noise, light, being too hot or cold, or an uncomfortable bed can make it harder to sleep.
What to do now
Watch this video on how to fall asleep faster and better to set your bedroom up for sleep success.
Food and drink
Drinks with caffeine, such as tea, coffee, cola or energy drinks, can keep you awake. Heavy meals or foods that are hard to digest can also affect sleep.
Alcohol is also not great for sleep. It can make you feel sleepy at first, but it can lead to poor sleep later in the night.
What to do now
The NHS recommends that you don't smoke or drink alcohol, tea or coffee at least 6 hours before going to bed.
Instead, try a warm milky drink or herbal tea.
If you're drinking alcohol during the week and would like to cut back, read our guide on how to drink less alcohol.
Some medicines
Some medicines can affect your sleep.
What to do now
Check the side effects listed in the leaflet included in your medicine box.
If you think your medicine may be causing sleep problems, speak to a GP or pharmacist.
Getting older
As we get older, our sleep patterns can change. Some people may sleep less at night or doze during the day.
Menopause or perimenopause can also affect your ability to fall asleep and stay asleep. Night sweats can make this even worse.
What to do now
If you think you have symptoms of menopause or perimenopause, and want to know what your options are, contact your GP.
Visit our page on how to sleep better to get tips on setting up a good sleep routine, which can help if your sleep has changed as you've got older.
Changes to your routine
Shift work, travel, illness or other changes to your routine can affect your sleep pattern.
What to do now
When you are ready to sleep, watch our video on beditation to help you relax and get your mind and body ready for rest.
Not moving enough during the day
If you are not active during the day, your body may not feel ready for sleep at night.
What to do now
Try this home workout video to help you build up to 150 minutes of exercise a week, which is recommended for adults.
However, the NHS advises avoiding exercise in the 4 hours before bed, so try to be active in the morning or early afternoon instead.
